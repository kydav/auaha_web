How to tell people you were laid off

This is the part people dread more than the job search itself. Here's what actually helps, roughly in the order you'll need it, with wording you can borrow.

Why it feels worse than it is

A layoff is a decision about a budget, a team or a business line. Being fired is a decision about you. Almost everyone knows the difference, and yet the feeling in your chest doesn't seem to.

Part of that is simply that work is where a lot of us keep our sense of being useful. When it goes, the instinct is to hide until you've fixed it — to come back with the new job already in hand, so the whole thing can be presented as a story with an ending. That instinct is understandable and it costs you, because the people who could help are the ones you're hiding from.

Telling your partner or family

Lead with the facts and the plan, in that order, because the person you're telling is going to be frightened about money and will feel better knowing you've thought about it — even if the plan is only a week long.

Something like: "I was laid off today. It's part of a bigger cut, it isn't about my performance. I get severance through the end of next month, I'm filing for unemployment tomorrow, and I'm going to spend this week working out what I'm aiming at before I start applying."

Then say the other part, which is harder and matters more: "I'm okay, but I'm not going to be great for a few days." People cope much better with a sad person who warned them than with one who seems fine and then isn't.

Telling your network

  • Be specific about what you want. "Let me know if you hear of anything" gives people nothing to act on. "I'm looking for backend roles at companies under 200 people, ideally remote" gives them something they can match against.
  • Ask for something small. An introduction, or fifteen minutes on a call. People say yes to small asks and then often do more than you asked.
  • Say it plainly. "My role was cut in a restructure" needs no apology attached to it. Adding one invites the other person to treat it as bad news about you.
  • Go wide early. The news is at its most useful in the first fortnight, while it's fresh and people's instinct to help is strongest.

A message you can send

"Hi — some news: my role was cut last week as part of a wider restructure at [company]. I'm fine, and I'm starting to look. I'm aiming at [the kind of role], ideally [the kind of company]. If anyone comes to mind, or you know of something, I'd really appreciate you keeping me in mind. Happy to send my CV over."

Short, unembarrassed, and specific enough that someone reading it can immediately think of a name. That's the whole job of the message.

Telling children

Children pick up on tension long before they're told anything, and what they invent is usually worse than the truth. Say it simply, say what won't change, and be honest that you don't know everything yet.

"My job ended, so I'm going to be home more for a while until I find a new one. We're okay. You'll still go to the same school and see the same friends." Then let them ask. It's normal for the questions to come days later, out of nowhere.

Frequently asked

Should I post about it on LinkedIn?

It works for a lot of people — layoff posts get unusual reach, and reach is exactly what you need. But only post when you can write it without bitterness. If you're not there yet, message twenty people individually instead. That's often more effective anyway.

What if I'm too embarrassed to tell anyone?

That feeling is close to universal, and it's worth knowing it's the layoff talking rather than a fact about your situation. Start with one person who you know will be kind. It gets substantially easier after the first time you say it out loud.

Do I have to tell people why I was laid off?

No. "My role was cut" is a complete answer. If the reason is straightforward — a funding round that didn't happen, a whole team closed — saying so can help, because it makes clear it wasn't about you.
