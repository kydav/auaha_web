How to explain a layoff in an interview

Interviewers ask about layoffs far less than candidates expect, and care far less than candidates fear. The risk isn't the layoff — it's the way people talk about it. Here's how to make it a non-event.

The shape of a good answer

Three sentences, and then move on. What happened, that it was structural, what you're looking for next. The brevity is doing most of the work: a short answer signals that you consider it unremarkable, and the interviewer takes their cue from you.

"My role was cut in November — the company reduced headcount by about a third and closed the team I was on. I'd been there three years and I'm proud of what we shipped. What I'm looking for now is somewhere I can keep doing [the thing], with a bit more [the thing you actually want]."

That's it. No apology, no explanation of the company's finances, no reassurance that it wasn't your fault. Reassurance offered before it's asked for reads as something to reassure about.

Three ways people make it worse

  • Talking too long. The single most common error. A ninety-second answer to a fifteen-second question tells the interviewer this is a sore subject and invites them to poke at it.
  • Criticising the old employer. Even when it's deserved, and it often is. The interviewer isn't assessing whether your old company behaved badly — they're imagining how you'll talk about them one day.
  • Pre-emptive defending. "It really wasn't about my performance, I had great reviews" answers a question nobody asked. Let the shortness of the answer do that work instead.

What about the gap?

Have one sentence for it and make sure that sentence contains something you did. "I took a few weeks to work out what I actually wanted rather than applying to everything, and I've been [learning X / consulting for Y / helping Z]." Deliberate reads completely differently from adrift, and the difference is often just whether you've articulated it.

Gaps are also far more normal than they were even a few years ago. Large, well-publicised layoffs have made "my role was cut" one of the least remarkable things you can say in an interview.

If you're carrying anger into the room

Sometimes the layoff genuinely was unfair — badly handled, poorly communicated, aimed at the wrong people. That anger is legitimate and it will also be audible in an interview, in ways you won't hear yourself.

The practical fix is to have said it all out loud somewhere else first, to a friend or on paper. The fifteen-minute values exercise on this site is useful here for a specific reason: it moves your attention off what the company decided and onto what you actually care about, which is the frame you want to be in when someone asks what you're looking for next.

Frequently asked

Should I bring up the layoff before they ask?

Only if there's an obvious gap on your CV, in which case mention it briefly and move on. Otherwise wait to be asked. Volunteering it makes it larger than it is.

Is 'laid off' better than 'made redundant' or 'restructured'?

Use whatever's normal where you are. What matters is that the phrasing is neutral and structural — about the role ending rather than about you being let go.

What if I was laid off more than once?

It's more common than you'd think, particularly if you've worked at startups or in a sector that contracted. Name the pattern before they wonder about it: "Two of the last three companies had layoffs — I've been aiming at early-stage, and that's part of the deal. It's why stability matters to me in this next one."
