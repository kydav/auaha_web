Plain answers

Laid off vs fired: what's the difference?

People use the two words interchangeably, and they shouldn't. The distinction changes what you're likely to be eligible for, what a former employer will confirm, and how the whole thing reads to a future employer.

The core difference

A layoff is the employer eliminating a position. The reason sits with the business — a budget, a restructure, a merger, a product being shut down, a funding round that didn't land. The work went away, and you went with it.

Being fired is the employer ending your employment because of you — performance, conduct, attendance, a policy breach. The role usually still exists afterwards, and somebody else will do it.

The plainest test: if your employer would hire someone else to do exactly your job next month, you were fired. If the job itself no longer exists, you were laid off.

Why the difference matters

  • Unemployment eligibility. Layoffs generally qualify. Being fired for misconduct generally doesn't, though rules vary by state and by what exactly happened.
  • What a former employer will say. Many companies confirm only dates and job title either way — but the internal record differs, and it can surface in background checks.
  • Severance. Layoffs often come with severance, notice pay, or extended benefits. Firings usually don't.
  • How it reads in an interview. "My role was cut" needs no defending. "I was let go" invites a follow-up question.

The blurry cases

Sometimes a company frames a performance exit as a layoff, to make it easier for everyone. Sometimes a genuine layoff is used to remove people the company wanted gone anyway. And sometimes you'll be offered the chance to resign instead.

Resigning is worth thinking about carefully, because it can affect unemployment eligibility — you generally left voluntarily, which is a different category. The trade is a cleaner story against a possible loss of benefits, and which way that trade goes depends on your finances, so it's worth a conversation with someone who knows your local rules before deciding.

Frequently asked

Is 'let go' the same as being fired?

"Let go" is deliberately vague and gets used for both. If you're describing your own situation, use the specific word — it's clearer and it's better for you if the accurate word is 'laid off'.

Can I get unemployment if I was fired?

Sometimes. Being dismissed for not being a good fit is treated differently from being dismissed for misconduct in most places. It's worth filing and letting the agency make the determination rather than assuming you're not eligible.

Will a future employer know which one it was?

Often not in any detail. Most large employers confirm only employment dates and title. But answer honestly if asked directly — being caught in a rewrite is far worse than either original reason.
