First week

You just got laid off. Here's the order to do things in.

The worst part of the first few days is that everything feels equally urgent. It isn't. Here's what genuinely needs handling this week, what can wait a fortnight, and the two things people most often get wrong while they're still in shock.

Before you sign anything

Most people are handed severance paperwork in the same meeting where they're told they're being let go — which is the worst possible moment to read a legal document. You are almost never required to sign on the spot, and in many cases you're entitled to time to review it.

Take the paperwork home. Read it when you can actually think. If the numbers are significant or there's a non-compete, non-disparagement or release of claims in there, it is worth paying an employment lawyer for a single consultation before you sign — that is usually a couple of hundred dollars against a decision that can be worth a great deal more.

The first 48 hours

  • Get your personal files off the laptop — legally. Your contacts, your portfolio, your reference letters. Not company data. Access is usually cut within hours, and often before you get home.
  • Write down what happened while it's fresh. Date, who was in the room, the reason given, what you were offered. You will want the specifics later, for unemployment claims and for interviews.
  • Check what happens to your health insurance and when. The end date is often the end of the month rather than your last day, but not always. This is the detail people miss.
  • File for unemployment. There's often a waiting period, so filing early matters. Being laid off — as distinct from being fired for cause — generally makes you eligible.
  • Tell two people. Not the internet. Two people who will be kind to you. That's enough for now.

The two things people get wrong

The first is applying immediately. Within a day or two of losing a job, most people fire off twenty applications, because doing something feels better than doing nothing. Those applications are written by someone in shock, and they read like it. They're also aimed at whatever happens to be listed rather than at what you actually want, which means the interviews you do get are for jobs you don't want.

The second is going quiet. Layoffs carry a shame that firings somehow don't — even though a layoff is, by definition, about the company's finances rather than your performance. People disappear for a few weeks to sort themselves out first. But your network is at its most useful in the first fortnight, when the news is fresh and people instinctively want to help.

What can wait

  • Rewriting your entire CV. You'll rewrite it again once you know what you're aiming at. A day or two of thinking first saves you doing it twice.
  • Deciding on a career change. Week one is the worst possible time to make a ten-year decision. Note the impulse, revisit it in a month.
  • Telling everyone. There's no deadline. Tell people as you're ready to.
  • A LinkedIn 'open to work' banner. Useful eventually. Not urgent, and not while you're still deciding what you're open to.

The thing worth doing early

There's one exercise with real evidence behind it, and it takes about fifteen minutes. In a field study run with a government employment agency and published in PNAS, unemployed people who wrote about their most important personal values received 49% more job offers over the following four weeks than those who didn't.

It isn't positive thinking and it isn't a productivity system. Losing a job is a threat to your sense of who you are, and the exercise works by reconnecting you to the parts of yourself the layoff didn't touch. It's free here, needs no account, and what you write never leaves your device.

Frequently asked

Should I say I was laid off or that I left?

Say you were laid off. It's verifiable, it's extremely common, and it carries no implication about your performance — a layoff is a decision about a role or a budget, not about you. Trying to dress it up as a resignation creates a story you then have to maintain.

How long does it usually take to find a new job?

It varies enormously by field and seniority, and any number you read is an average hiding a very wide range. What's more useful to know is that the first few weeks feel disproportionately bleak because nothing has had time to come back yet — most processes take weeks to produce a reply.

Should I take any job to stop the gap?

A short gap is far less damaging than people fear, and far less damaging than a role you leave after three months. If money makes it necessary, take the work — but treat that as a financial decision rather than a CV one.
