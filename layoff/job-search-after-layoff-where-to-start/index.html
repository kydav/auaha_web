The search

Starting a job search after a layoff

Most people start by applying to everything, get very few replies, and conclude something is wrong with them. Usually the problem is the shape of the search, not the person doing it.

Decide what you're aiming at first

A day spent working out what you're actually looking for will save you a fortnight of unfocused applications. You want three things written down: the kind of role, the kind of company, and your genuine constraints on money and location.

This feels like procrastinating and isn't. Every part of the search downstream — your CV, what you say in a call, who you ask for introductions — gets sharper once you can finish the sentence "I'm looking for…" without hedging.

Where offers actually come from

  • People who already know you. Former colleagues and managers. The highest hit rate of anything, by a wide margin, and the most underused because it feels like asking for a favour.
  • Second-degree introductions. Someone who knows someone. Nearly as good, and easier to ask for than a job.
  • Applying directly to specific companies. Works, particularly at smaller companies where a real person reads it.
  • Mass applications through job boards. Lowest hit rate by far. Worth doing, worth not making the whole strategy.

How many applications a day

Fewer than you think, done better. Five carefully targeted applications a week will out-perform fifty generic ones, and the difference isn't effort — it's that the targeted ones are readable as being from a specific person who wants that specific job.

Structure the week instead of the day: two mornings on applications, one on outreach and conversations, one on preparation or learning, and genuinely take the rest off. A job search that fills every waking hour produces worse work and a worse-sounding candidate, and there's nowhere to put the exhaustion.

The part nobody schedules

Rejection arrives in clusters, often weeks after you'd stopped thinking about the role. It compounds quietly, and by week six most people are applying from a much worse psychological place than they started in — which shows in cover letters and in interviews.

This is the actual argument for doing the values exercise early, and revisiting it. The published study found that people who did it received more job offers over the following four weeks, and the mechanism is fairly intuitive: someone who is clear about what they're worth applies differently from someone who isn't.

Frequently asked

Should I use recruiters?

Yes, as one channel of several. Recruiters work for the employer, not for you, which is worth remembering — but a good one in your field is genuinely useful, and worth building a relationship with even between jobs.

Should I lower my salary expectations?

Not at the start. You can always come down; you can't come back up in the same process. If the search stretches on, revisit it as a deliberate decision with a number attached rather than drifting downward under pressure.

How do I stay motivated after a lot of rejections?

Track the things you control — conversations had, applications sent, people contacted — rather than only outcomes, which arrive late and unevenly. And keep at least one part of the week that isn't about the search at all.
