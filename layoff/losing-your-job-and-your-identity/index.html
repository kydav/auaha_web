The part nobody prepares you for

It isn't just the money. Here's why it hurts.

People expect a layoff to be financially frightening. What surprises almost everyone is how much of the difficulty isn't about money at all — and there's a decent body of research explaining why, along with one intervention that's actually been tested.

Why it hits where it does

For a lot of people, work carries far more than income. It carries the answer to what you say at parties, a daily structure, the sense of being useful to somebody, and a large part of the evidence you use to decide whether you're doing well in life.

Losing the job takes all of it at once, on a day you didn't choose. Psychologists call this an identity threat — an event that undermines your sense of yourself as competent and worthwhile. Which is why the grief is disproportionate to the paycheque, and why 'but you'll find something else' lands so poorly.

What self-affirmation actually means

The term sounds like standing in front of a mirror telling yourself you're great. In the research literature it means something narrower and more useful: when one part of your self-image takes a hit, you can restore your sense of integrity by reconnecting with other parts of it that are untouched.

You didn't stop being a good parent when the role was cut. You didn't stop being the person your friends call. Your judgement, your craft, the thing you'd still do on a Saturday — none of that was in the redundancy pool. The exercise is a structured way of remembering that at a moment when it's genuinely hard to.

It's been tested on actual job seekers

In 2023, researchers published two field experiments in PNAS on exactly this. Unemployed people spent ten to fifteen minutes writing about the values that mattered most to them and why. That was the whole intervention — once, not daily.

In the second study, run with a government employment agency, 10.9% of the people who did the exercise were re-employed within four weeks, against 3.4% of those who didn't. They also received about 49% more job offers. In the first study the increase in offers was larger still.

Two honest caveats, because they matter. The effect on re-employment and offers was measured at four weeks and was no longer statistically significant at eight. And the difference in how quickly people found work was about a day — the meaningful result is offers, not speed.

When it's more than a bad few weeks

Job loss carries a real, documented risk of depression, and there's a difference between the flat, grey weeks that are a normal response and something that needs support. If you're not sleeping for weeks, can't function, or find yourself thinking about not being here, please talk to a doctor or a therapist.

In the US you can call or text 988 at any time. This page isn't therapy and neither is the exercise — it's fifteen minutes of writing with some evidence behind it, which is a useful thing and not a substitute for care.

Frequently asked

Is this the same as positive affirmations?

No, and the difference is the point. Affirmations are statements about yourself you repeat. This is a writing exercise about what you value and why, and it's the version that's been tested in field experiments with unemployed people.

Do I have to do it more than once?

The study administered it once. Whether repeating it extends the benefit hasn't been tested — the authors say so explicitly. Do it once, and again later if it helped.

Does what I write get sent anywhere?

No. The writing stays in your browser on your own device. We record only anonymous counts — that an exercise was started or finished — so we know whether the page is useful to people. We never see a word of it.
