Who Pays the Buyer's Agent Now? A Plain-English Guide to Post-Settlement Commissions

Published August 21, 2026 · By the Threshold team · ~6 min read

Since the 2024 NAR settlement, there's no automatic commission waiting for the buyer's agent in the listing. So who actually pays it? Here's the honest, jargon-free answer — for the buyer, the seller, and the agent who has to explain it at the kitchen table.

The short answer
  • There are three ways a buyer's agent gets paid: the seller/listing broker covers it, the buyer pays directly, or a seller concession is credited toward the buyer.
  • No one has to pay it automatically anymore — it's a negotiation on every deal.
  • Commissions are not set by law and are fully negotiable.
  • Wherever the money comes from, the number is agreed in the written buyer agreement.

If you've bought or sold a home in the last couple of years, you've probably run into some version of this confusion:

"My contract states that the seller authorized the broker to offer compensation to the buyer's broker… Can I refuse to do that? I don't see why I, the seller, have to pay the buyer's agent a commission." — a seller, describing exactly the knot this guide untangles

It's a fair question, and the short version is: you don't have to. But to see why, it helps to understand what changed in 2024 — because almost every "who pays the buyer's agent" argument today is downstream of that one shift.

What actually changed in 2024

As part of the National Association of REALTORS® (NAR) settlement, two big things took effect on August 17, 2024:

  • Offers of buyer-agent compensation came off the MLS. Listings can no longer advertise a "we'll pay the buyer's agent X%" number through the Multiple Listing Service. That old, semi-automatic offer is gone.
  • Buyers now sign a written agreement up front. Agents who work through an MLS must have a signed buyer agreement — stating a specific, objective compensation amount — before touring a home with a buyer.

Put those together and you get today's reality: buyer-agent pay is decoupled. It's no longer assumed to come from the seller. It's negotiated, deal by deal, and written down.

The three ways a buyer's agent gets paid

Whenever someone asks "who pays the buyer's agent," the honest answer is "it depends what you negotiate." In practice it lands in one of three buckets:

1. The seller or listing broker pays

The seller can still choose to offer compensation to the buyer's agent — many do, because it widens the pool of buyers who can afford the home. This is now negotiated off the MLS (directly, or as a term of the offer) rather than advertised in the listing. When this happens, it typically doesn't come out of the buyer's pocket at closing.

2. The buyer pays their own agent directly

If the seller isn't covering it, the buyer pays their agent the amount they agreed to in their buyer agreement. The buyer can still ask the seller to contribute as part of the offer — but if the seller says no, the obligation is the buyer's.

3. A seller concession is credited toward the buyer

A middle path: the seller agrees to a concession — a credit toward the buyer's costs — which the buyer can then apply to their agent's fee, closing costs, or both. Economically it's close to the seller paying, but it runs through the buyer's side of the settlement statement.

So, seller — do you have to pay the buyer's agent?

No. That's the direct answer to the question at the top. As a seller you can:

  1. Offer compensation up front to make your home more attractive to buyers and their agents.
  2. Negotiate it as part of an offer — treat it like any other term you're trading on price and concessions.
  3. Decline entirely, in which case the buyer covers their own agent under their buyer agreement.

None of these is "the rule." They're options. The right one depends on your market, how motivated buyers are, and what a strong offer is worth to you. If offering to cover the buyer's agent brings you a better, faster offer, it may be worth it — but that's a business decision, not a legal obligation.

Where the number really lives: the buyer agreement

Here's the piece that ties it all together. No matter who ends up paying, the buyer agent's fee is agreed in the written buyer agreement — signed before touring homes together, stating a specific, objective amount. That document is the source of truth. It's what lets an agent say, cleanly, "here's my fee, here's who we'll ask to cover it, and here's what happens if they don't."

Frequently asked questions

Who pays the buyer's agent after the NAR settlement?

It depends on what's negotiated. The three options are: the seller/listing broker covers it, the buyer pays their agent directly, or a seller concession is credited toward the buyer. There's no automatic, MLS-published offer anymore — the amount is set in the buyer agreement.

As a seller, do I have to pay the buyer's agent?

No. You can offer compensation to attract buyers, negotiate it as part of an offer, or decline — in which case the buyer covers their own agent. It's a negotiation, not an obligation.

Are real estate commissions set by law?

No. Commissions and buyer-agent compensation are not set by law and are fully negotiable between each agent and their client.

Where is the buyer agent's fee actually agreed?

In the written buyer agreement. For agents working through an MLS, it must state a specific, objective amount and be signed before touring a home together — whoever ends up paying it.
