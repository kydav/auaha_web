Buyer Commission Is a Negotiated Offer Term Now Nail It in the Buyer Agreement First

Published August 21, 2026 · By the Threshold team · ~6 min read

Buyer-agent compensation used to travel quietly with the listing. Now it's an explicit line in the offer — negotiated in the open. Which means the cleanest deals start well before the offer: at the buyer agreement.

The short answer
  • Buyer-agent compensation is now an explicit term of the offer, alongside price and concessions.
  • It flows from the buyer agreement — so settle the number there, before you write.
  • A signed, specific amount means no mid-deal renegotiation of your own fee.
  • If the seller won't cover it, the gap is a known quantity you can plan around, not a surprise.

Agents are watching a new pattern play out on the buyer side:

"Buyers are offering 8–10% under asking, plus full closing costs and buyer's-agent commission, then refusing to negotiate." — a listing agent describing the new shape of offers

Notice what's in that sentence: buyer-agent commission sitting right alongside price and closing costs, as a thing being asked for in the offer. That's the shift. Compensation is no longer an assumption baked into the listing — it's a negotiated term. And terms that show up in offers should be settled before you write, not improvised at the table.

Why compensation became an offer term

After the 2024 NAR settlement, the buyer agrees — in writing, up front — to pay their agent a specific amount. The seller is no longer presumed to cover it. So when a buyer wants the seller to pay some or all of that fee, that ask has to go somewhere: into the offer, as a term, right next to price and concessions. It's the same logic as asking for a closing-cost credit. Everything is on the table, and everything is explicit.

The problem with settling it late

If the compensation number isn't nailed down before you write, you're negotiating two things at once — the deal and your own pay — often under time pressure. That's where it gets messy:

  • The buyer is surprised by a gap between what they owe you and what the seller will cover.
  • Your fee looks like it's "up for grabs" in the middle of the transaction.
  • You're reworking the offer late instead of presenting a clean, confident number.

Nail it in the buyer agreement first

The fix is upstream. Because MLS-based agents must have a signed buyer agreement before touring anyway, that document is the natural place to lock the number. Do it there and the offer stage becomes simple math:

  1. State a specific, objective amount in the buyer agreement — the fee your client has agreed to pay you, not an open-ended placeholder.
  2. Frame the seller ask as a term. When you write, you request the seller cover some or all of that known amount — clearly, like any other term.
  3. Handle any gap on purpose. If the seller covers less than the agreed fee, you already know the exact shortfall and can plan for it: a concession, an adjustment, or the buyer paying the difference.

The number never wobbles. You're not renegotiating your compensation deal by deal — you're applying a figure your client already signed off on.

What "nailing it" gives your buyer

This isn't only about protecting your fee. A buyer who signed a clear number up front knows exactly what they're responsible for and what you're asking the seller to cover. When the offer goes in, there's no "wait, I owe how much?" moment. That clarity is a trust-builder — the opposite of the mid-deal surprise that sours buyer-agent relationships.

Frequently asked questions

Is buyer-agent commission part of the offer now?

Effectively yes. Because the buyer agrees to pay their agent a set amount in the buyer agreement, any request for the seller to cover it becomes an explicit term of the offer — like price or concessions.

Should I sign the buyer agreement before writing an offer?

Yes. MLS-based agents must sign it before touring anyway, and settling the compensation there means it flows cleanly into every offer. Fixing the number at offer time invites confusion and last-minute renegotiation.

What happens if the seller won't cover the buyer's agent fee?

The buyer covers the difference under their agreement. Because the amount is already in writing, you can present the shortfall clearly and decide how to handle it — a concession, an adjustment, or the buyer paying the gap — without renegotiating your fee mid-deal.

Does the buyer agreement have to state a specific compensation amount?

Yes. For MLS-based agents, the written buyer agreement must state a specific, objective amount — not open-ended — signed before touring a home together.
