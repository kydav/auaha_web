Commission Splits Are Negotiable Now How to Read a Listing Agreement in the Post-NAR World

Published August 21, 2026 · By the Threshold team · ~6 min read

"The fee structure pays the listing agent 3.5% and offers the buyer's agent 2% — is that split abnormal?" It's one of the most common questions sellers ask now. The honest answer reframes the whole thing: there's no "normal" anymore, only what you negotiate.

The short answer
  • There is no standard split. Commissions aren't set by law or by the MLS — they're negotiated.
  • An uneven split (e.g. 3.5% / 2%) isn't "abnormal," it's just one negotiated outcome.
  • Buyer-side offers are no longer published in the MLS — they're negotiated off-MLS or in the offer.
  • The real question isn't "is this normal?" but "is the buyer-side number competitive in my market?"
"The fee structure pays the selling agent 3.5% and offers the buying agent 2%… Is this split abnormal? Will it cause a problem attracting buyers?" — a seller reading a listing agreement for the first time post-settlement

This question comes up constantly, and it hides an assumption worth retiring: that there's a "correct" split a listing agreement is supposed to match. Since the 2024 NAR settlement, that assumption no longer holds. Let's walk through what a split actually is now, and how to read the agreement in front of you.

Why "abnormal" is the wrong lens

Commissions have always been legally negotiable — but for years, MLS conventions made a roughly even split feel like the default. The settlement removed the mechanism that reinforced that habit. So a 3.5% / 2% arrangement isn't breaking a rule; there's no rule to break. It's simply the number the seller and listing agent agreed on, and the number the seller might offer toward a buyer's agent.

The more useful frame is competitiveness, not normalcy. Ask: given how buyers in my market are being represented right now, is a 2% buyer-side offer enough to keep my home attractive?

What decoupling changed

Two 2024 changes reshaped how splits work:

  • Buyer-side offers came off the MLS. You can no longer advertise buyer-agent compensation in the listing. Any offer to a buyer's agent is negotiated separately or as part of the offer.
  • Buyers sign their own agreement. Buyers now agree, in writing and up front, to pay their agent a specific amount. If the seller-side offer is lower than that amount, the buyer may have to cover the gap.

That second point is the crux. In the old world, a "low" co-op offer mostly affected the buyer's agent. Now it can land on the buyer — and buyers feel every dollar of a gap between what their agreement says they owe and what the seller is willing to contribute.

Will a lower buyer-side number scare off buyers?

It can — but it's a trade-off, not a disqualifier. If a buyer's agreement says the buyer owes 2.5% and the seller offers 2%, that half-point becomes something the buyer must find: pay it out of pocket, ask for it back as a concession, or move on to a home where the math is cleaner. A well-priced, in-demand home absorbs this more easily than one that's already stretching. This is exactly the kind of thing to weigh with your agent, deal by deal.

How to actually read the listing agreement

When a listing agreement lands in front of you, check these, and remember every one is negotiable:

  1. Total commission and how it's described. Is it a single figure to the listing brokerage, or split into a listing side and a buyer side?
  2. Any buyer-side offer. Is compensation to a cooperating buyer's agent designated — and is it framed as an obligation or an option you can adjust per offer?
  3. Term and cancellation. How long does the agreement run, and how can you exit if it's not working?
  4. How a buyer-side request is handled. If a buyer asks you to cover their agent, does the agreement give you room to negotiate that at offer time?

For agents: this is a conversation, not a form field

If you list homes, the split is now something you explain and justify, not something the MLS quietly set for you. Sellers will ask "is this normal?" — and the strongest answer is to reframe it around their goal: how to price the buyer-side offer so it attracts strong buyers without giving away margin. And on the buyer side, the same clarity applies to your own fee: it's negotiated with your client and captured in the buyer agreement, so there's never a mystery about the number.

Frequently asked questions

Is a 3.5% listing / 2% buyer-side split abnormal?

No. There's no standard split set by law or the MLS. Commissions are fully negotiable, and an uneven split is just what the seller and listing agent negotiated. What matters is whether the buyer-side amount is competitive in your market.

Will a lower buyer-side commission hurt my chances of attracting buyers?

It can. Because buyers now agree to pay their own agent, a buyer-side offer below that agreed amount can leave the buyer to cover the gap — which may narrow your buyer pool or surface during negotiation. It's a trade-off to weigh, not a fixed rule.

Are commission splits still published in the MLS?

No. Since August 17, 2024, offers of buyer-agent compensation aren't allowed on the MLS. Buyer-side compensation is negotiated off-MLS or as a term of the offer, and the buyer's own fee is set in their buyer agreement.

What should I look for in a listing agreement now?

The total commission and how it's described, whether any amount is designated for a buyer's agent, whether that's an obligation or an option, the term and cancellation rights, and how a buyer-side request would be handled. All negotiable.
