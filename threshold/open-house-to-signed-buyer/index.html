From open house to signed buyer: what to say in the first 90 seconds

Published September 9, 2026 · By the Threshold team · ~6 min read

You gave up a Sunday, you got fourteen names on the sheet, and not one of them has called you back. The problem usually isn't your follow-up. It's that the conversation that turns a visitor into a client never happened while they were standing in front of you.

The short version
  • An open house is the one place you meet unrepresented buyers with nothing to sign — which is exactly why nothing compels them to commit.
  • Most agents lose them by deferring the ask to a follow-up email.
  • The agreement can be limited in scope and duration — a small ask converts where a big one doesn't.
  • Whatever you agree, it has to be signed — which is a paperwork problem you can solve on the spot instead of at the office.

Here's a version of the complaint that shows up constantly in agent forums:

"I thought I could do open houses to find people to work with, but that's not the case. I've held a bunch of open houses and still haven't picked up a client." — a newer agent, on r/realtors

The open house isn't the problem. Open houses still put you in a room with buyers who have no agent, no obligation, and an active interest in property. That's a better prospecting surface than almost anything you can buy. What fails is the handoff: you collect a name and a phone number, they leave unrepresented, and by the time your follow-up lands they've met someone else at the next open house down the road.

First, be accurate about what the rules require

Since the 2024 NAR settlement, an agent who is a NAR member needs a written buyer agreement in place before touring homes with a buyer. Open houses are generally treated as outside that: someone walking through a public open house is not touring with you as their agent, and you don't need their signature to let them in the door. Several state rules that landed afterward carve out open houses explicitly too, and brokerages set their own policies on top.

This matters for two reasons. The first is that getting it wrong in front of a client is expensive — if you tell a visitor the law requires them to sign before they can look around, you've said something untrue to someone who can check, and you've made the ask feel like a trick. The second is more useful: because nothing forces the signature, everything depends on the conversation. That's the part you control.

The 90 seconds that decide it

The window is short and it starts the moment someone stops pretending to look at the kitchen. Roughly:

  1. Find out where they actually are. Not "are you working with an agent?" — which invites a defensive no — but "how long have you been looking?" and "seen anything close?" You learn whether they're three weeks or nine months in, and whether anyone is already helping them.
  2. Give them something before you ask for anything. One specific, useful thing about this house or this street that they couldn't have got from the listing: what it last sold for, why it's priced where it is, what the inspection on the one two doors down turned up. This is the whole play. You're demonstrating the service rather than describing it.
  3. Name the fork honestly. "I can keep doing that for you — pulling the history, telling you when something's overpriced. To actually show you homes I need us to have an agreement in place. That's the rule now, and it protects you as much as me." Said plainly, this reads as competence.
  4. Make the ask small. Offer a limited agreement — a short window, or this property only — rather than leading with a long exclusive. Most buyers aren't refusing to work with you; they're refusing to promise a stranger six months.
  5. Sign it while you're standing there. The moment you say "I'll email it over," you've converted a warm conversation into an item on someone's to-do list, competing with their actual job.

Why "I'll send the paperwork over" loses

Every hour between the conversation and the signature is an hour in which the reason to sign gets weaker. The buyer's enthusiasm was about the house they were standing in. By Tuesday evening the house is abstract, the agent is a name in an inbox, and the PDF needs printing, signing and scanning — three obstacles between an interested buyer and a client.

This is also why the agents who convert open houses well tend to be the ones who've made signing physically trivial. Not because paperwork is persuasive, but because the ask has to happen at the moment of interest, and anything that pushes it later is functionally a refusal.

Make the agreement small enough to say yes to

Scope, duration and exclusivity are all negotiable, and treating them as fixed is a self-inflicted wound. A one-property agreement, or a two-week non-exclusive, gives a hesitant buyer a way to say yes that costs them almost nothing — and gives you a real relationship to convert later. An agent who only offers the six-month exclusive is choosing a lower conversion rate in exchange for terms most of those buyers were never going to accept from someone they met forty minutes ago.

Your brokerage will have a view on which forms and terms you can offer, so have that conversation before the next open house rather than in front of a buyer.

Common questions

Do buyers need to sign a buyer agreement to attend an open house?

Generally no. The NAR settlement requires a written buyer agreement before an agent tours homes with a buyer, and open houses are widely treated as outside that — a visitor walking through a public open house is not touring with you as their agent. Some state rules and brokerage policies add their own requirements, so confirm with your broker. The practical point is that nothing compels the signature at an open house: you have to earn it.

How do I ask an open-house visitor to sign without scaring them off?

Lead with value rather than paperwork, then be straight about the fork in the road: you can keep helping them or you can't, and the agreement is what makes the difference. Offer a limited agreement — one property or a short window — so the ask is small. The pressure people react badly to usually comes from a long exclusive agreement presented as a formality.

Can a buyer agreement be limited to one property or a short time?

Yes. Scope, duration and exclusivity are all negotiable, and a short or single-property agreement is a normal way to start a relationship that neither side is sure about yet. Your brokerage may have its own policy on which forms and terms you can use, so check before you offer one.

Why do open houses produce so few clients?

Because most agents collect contact details and follow up later. The visitor leaves unrepresented, meets someone else, and the follow-up arrives after the decision has already been made. The conversion happens in the conversation, not in the CRM.
