What the driving actually costs you

Every farrier knows fuel is expensive. Far fewer know what a mile actually costs them once the truck, the tires and the unpaid hour behind the wheel are counted — and that number is the difference between a full book that pays and a full book that just keeps you busy.

Fuel is only part of the bill

Fuel is the number you watch because you hand over a card for it. But a working rig costs you on every mile whether you notice or not — tires, brakes, oil, the transmission, and the resale value quietly draining out of a truck loaded with an anvil and a forge.

The simplest honest estimate is the IRS standard mileage rate, which exists precisely to bundle fuel, maintenance and depreciation into one per-mile figure. Look up the current year's rate, multiply it by the miles you drove last month, and compare it to what you assumed the driving cost. For most farriers it is meaningfully more.

The bill you never send

The bigger cost usually isn't the truck at all — it's the hour. Nobody pays you to drive. If you shoe six horses in a day and spend two hours between barns, those two hours come straight out of your effective hourly rate, and they don't show up anywhere in your books.

Work out your own number: take a normal day's earnings and divide it by the hours from leaving the house to getting home, not the hours with a hoof in your lap. The gap between that figure and what you think you charge per horse is what the driving is costing you. Once you've seen it, an hour of backtracking stops feeling free.

Where the wasted miles hide

  • Backtracking. Two clients in the same valley booked on different days, or in the wrong order on the same day — the most common and most fixable source of extra miles.
  • Days that aren't clustered. When you book by who called rather than who's nearby, the route is set by chance. Grouping due horses by area before you fill the day changes the whole shape of it.
  • Trips that didn't need to happen. An unconfirmed stop, a horse in the wrong field, a client who forgot — a wasted drive costs the fuel and the slot you could have sold.
  • Squeezing in the odd one out. The single horse an hour past your usual patch rarely pays for the round trip once you count the time. It's worth knowing which stops those are.

Frequently asked

How much does driving cost a farrier per mile?

More than the fuel alone. The usual way to estimate the full figure — fuel, maintenance, tires and depreciation together — is the IRS standard mileage rate for the current year, multiplied by the miles you actually drive. A loaded rig sits at the expensive end of that range, and the unpaid time behind the wheel is on top of it.

How do I work out what my time on the road is worth?

Divide a normal day's earnings by the total hours from leaving home to getting back, rather than the hours you spend working on horses. That's your real hourly rate. Comparing it to what you charge per horse shows exactly what the driving is taking.

How can farriers reduce time and fuel spent driving?

Group clients by area rather than booking in the order people call, order each day's stops geographically, and confirm appointments so you aren't making trips for nothing. Shoein' maps your whole client list, flags who's due, and optimizes the day's route so the miles follow the work instead of the other way round.

Should I charge a travel fee?

Many farriers do, either as a call-out fee or a per-mile charge beyond a set radius. The important part is knowing your real cost per mile and per hour first — a travel fee set below what the trip costs you is just a discount you didn't intend to give.
